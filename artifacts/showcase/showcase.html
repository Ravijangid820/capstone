Why this is hard

The data exists. It just cannot be moved.

A segmentation model wants every scan it can get. Patient-privacy law, ethics approval and institutional policy all say the scans stay where they were acquired. So each hospital trains alone on a fraction of the data — and each one plateaus early.

The silo

Four sites, roughly 250 training cases each. Alone, none of them sees enough variety to generalize well.

The pool

Pooling all four gives about 1,000 cases and a visibly better model — but it requires the exact data transfer that is not allowed.

The gap

That difference is the prize. In this study it is worth — Dice, and federated learning is the attempt to collect it without moving anything.

The honest framing. Centralized training is not a competitor here — it is the ceiling we are trying to approach. Local-only training is the floor we have to beat to justify any of this.
The mechanism

Send the model, not the data.

One communication round: the server ships the current weights to every site, each site trains on its own scans for one epoch, and only the updated weights travel back. No image ever leaves the hospital.

01
Broadcast
The server sends identical weights to all four sites.
02
Train locally
Each site runs one epoch on its own cases. Scans never move.
03
Return weights
Only parameter tensors go back — no images, no labels.
04
Aggregate
The server averages them into the next global model.
05
Repeat
Forty rounds in the final recipe.

What this buys

Every site's gradient signal reaches every other site's model, without a single scan being transferred.

What it costs

The averaged model is a compromise. It fits the majority of sites well — and that is exactly where the interesting failure lives.

The dataset

BraTS 2021 — 1,251 annotated glioma cases.

Every case is a four-channel MRI volume with a voxel-level expert segmentation. That is the raw material for everything that follows.

One case, in numbers

Four co-registered, skull-stripped modalities at 240 × 240 × 155 voxels, plus one label volume. The model reads all four channels stacked and predicts three overlapping masks.

Why segmentation, not classification

Clinical value is in where and how much, not just whether. Tumour volume drives treatment planning and response assessment — so the target is a mask, and the metric is overlap.

Input · 4 channels

The same brain, imaged four ways.

Each MRI sequence makes different tissue bright. No single one shows the whole tumour, which is why the model receives all four stacked as channels.

Case —, axial slice —. Rendered from the source volumes by the project's own loader — the same function every training run calls.
Target · 3 nested regions

Three masks, and each one contains the next.

All three regionsWT ⊃ TC ⊃ ET
Show

WT · Whole tumour

Everything abnormal — oedema, core, enhancing rim. The largest and easiest region, and the one every headline Dice figure in this project refers to.

TC · Tumour core

The solid mass inside the oedema: necrosis plus enhancing tissue. This is what a surgeon plans around.

ET · Enhancing tumour

Contrast-taking tissue only — the smallest, hardest region, and the most sensitive to a model's calibration.

Because the regions nest, they are predicted as three independent sigmoids, not a softmax. A softmax would force them to compete for the same voxel, which is the wrong relationship.
The federation

1,251 cases, split into four hospitals.

The cases are partitioned once, deterministically, and the manifest is committed to the repository. Every run in every iteration reads the same split — so no result can be an accident of who got which patient.

Held out, and left alone

62 test cases per site — 248 in total — are scored but never trained on. Every Dice figure in this deck comes from those volumes.

A real validation set

From v2 onward, 20 cases per site are taken for validation and used to choose which round's checkpoint to keep. They are drawn after the training cap, so the training set is unchanged.

Naming. The code keys hospitals H1–H4; all prose in this project calls them Site A–D, because H1–H3 also name the three hypotheses. Site D is the outlier.
The non-IID source

Four sites, four scanners.

Real hospitals differ because their machines differ. We simulate that with a fixed per-site transform — contrast gamma, a smooth spatial bias field, and mild blur. Sites A through C are mild and cluster together. Site D is the outlier.

Why nonlinear, and why spatial

Preprocessing z-normalizes every volume, which would erase a simple brightness or scale offset. A gamma curve plus a spatial bias field plus blur survive normalization — so the heterogeneity is still there when the model sees it. That was a deliberate design requirement, not a detail.

One subtlety that matters. The brain mask and crop box are computed on the unshifted volume. Blur smears brain intensity into the exactly-zero background, so a mask taken after the shift would grow with the site's blur — Site D's by 57% — and the crop shape would leak the site identity.
Preprocessing

Seven steps, in a load-bearing order.

Every case passes through the same chain before any model sees it. The order is not cosmetic — two of these steps only work correctly where they sit.

Why the mask comes first

Steps 02 and 04 exist purely to stop the blur halo from changing the crop. Without them the volume shape would correlate with the site — a model could read the site off the tensor dimensions and the whole experiment would be confounded.

Why normalise per case

Per-case z-scoring is standard for BraTS and removes scanner gain. It is also what forces the synthetic shift to be nonlinear: anything linear would be normalised straight back out.

Caching

Preprocess once. Train a hundred times.

Decoding and preprocessing a case takes seconds; a training run touches every case dozens of times. So the entire pipeline output is written to disk as fp16 tensors, keyed by a hash of the settings that produced it.

The key is the point

The cache key hashes the shift parameters and the clip threshold. Change the scanner model and you get a new cache, not a silently stale one — the failure mode where a run reads tensors built under different rules cannot happen.

What is stored per case

The cropped, normalised, clipped four-channel image; the three region masks; and the tumour bounding box, so tumour-biased sampling does not have to scan the volume every time it draws.

Sampling

Two backbones, two ways to cut a brain.

The whole study runs twice — once on 2D axial slices, once on 3D patches. The two are not a redundancy: they disagree, and where they disagree is a finding.

2D · axial slices

8 slices per case per epoch, cropped to 192 × 192, batch of 8. Cheap, many samples per epoch, and full in-plane context.

Loses through-plane continuity — the model never sees that a lesion continues into the next slice.

3D · cubic patches

2 patches per case per epoch, 96³ voxels, batch of 1. True volumetric context, evaluated with a sliding window at 0.25 overlap.

Far fewer samples per epoch, so each epoch is a much noisier estimate — which turns out to matter a great deal.

Tumour-biased sampling. With probability 0.7 a draw is forced to contain tumour. Uniform sampling would spend most of its budget on empty brain, and the Dice gradient on an all-background patch is nearly flat.
The network

One U-Net, written once, compiled for either dimension.

A residual U-Net with a shared implementation — the same code path builds the 2D and 3D models, so a difference between backbones is never a difference between two codebases.

Why BatchNorm is the whole story

Those 2,310 parameters — one seventh of one percent of the network — are the only ones FedBN keeps local. Everything this project concludes about personalization turns on that fraction.

Why fp32, deliberately

Mixed precision was tested and rejected: it corrupts BatchNorm running statistics. In a study whose central variable is the BatchNorm layer, a speed-up that quietly perturbs it is not a speed-up.

Four training regimes

Two references, and two real candidates.

Centralized

All 920 training cases pooled into one model.

The ceiling. Not deployable — it needs the data transfer that is forbidden.

Local-only

Each site trains its own model. Nothing is shared.

The floor. Anything federated must beat this or it is not worth the machinery.

FedAvg

Every weight averaged each round. One global model serves all four sites.

The standard baseline.

FedBN

Everything averaged except BatchNorm. Each site keeps its own normalisation.

A shared body, four local calibrations.

Matched compute, on purpose. Local-only and centralized train for rounds × local epochs total — the same number of epochs a federated site sees. Otherwise "collaboration helps" would just mean "we trained longer".
Pre-registered

Three hypotheses, written down before the runs.

H1Collaboration
Federating beats training alone, on average.

mean Dice (FedAvg) ≥ mean Dice (Local-only)

H2The outlier penalty
A single global model fails the site that differs most.

Dice (FedAvg, Site D) < Dice (Local-only, Site D)

H3Personalization
Keeping normalisation local recovers that site.

mean (FedBN) ≥ mean (FedAvg) and Dice (FedBN, D) ≥ Dice (FedAvg, D)

Stating these in advance is what makes the answers meaningful. A hypothesis chosen after seeing the numbers is a description, not a test — and two of these did not come out the way we expected.
Protocol

Most of this project is about not fooling ourselves.

Early on we found the round-to-round noise was larger than the effects we were trying to measure. Everything below exists because of that.

The estimator, fixed in advance

A headline figure is the mean of the final five rounds, never a single round. A single round can move a method by more than the difference between methods — we have a case where a single-round reading reverses the sign of the conclusion.

Per-case, paired

Dice is recorded for every one of the 248 test volumes individually. Comparisons are paired on the same case, with a bootstrap 95% CI over 10,000 resamples and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The sign split is reported

Alongside every mean shift we report how many volumes moved each way. A large mean driven by a handful of cases is a different claim from a consistent win, and the split makes the difference visible.

Frozen and hash-verified

Every iteration is archived with a SHA-256 manifest, and a checker re-derives sixteen headline figures from those logs. A document that disagrees with the archive fails the build.

Backbone
What we established

Three hypotheses, answered honestly.

H1 is the interesting one. Under v1, FedAvg beat training alone. Under v5 — with 230 training cases per site instead of 150 — plain FedAvg no longer does, in 2D. More local data raised the floor faster than it raised the average. FedBN still clears it, which is the argument for personalization rather than for federation as such.
Limits & next

What this study does not show.

Single seed at v3 and v5

The final recipe was run once per method. v1 and v2 have three seeds in 2D; the later iterations do not. Seed variance is the most likely explanation for any margin under about 0.01.

Recipe and data size are confounded

v3 raised the training cap from 150 to 230 cases and changed augmentation. One run at v5 with the old cap would separate them; it has not been done.

The shift is synthetic

Gamma, bias field and blur are a defensible model of scanner variation, not a substitute for four real hospitals.

Next · privacy

The study is federated but has no formal privacy guarantee. Adding differential privacy with (ε, δ) accounting would test a real prediction: DP noise should hurt Site D most, and FedBN — whose normalisation never enters the noisy aggregate — should degrade more gracefully than FedAvg.

Next · an unseen hospital

Every test site was in training. Holding one out entirely measures the question a deploying hospital actually asks.

Next · a stronger competitor

FedProx targets client heterogeneity directly. It is the natural rival to FedBN and would show whether personalization is the best answer or merely the first one we tried.
